Explain Artificial Intelligence Review, 2026 Published
~16,000

Why does the same AI training work in one school and fail in the next?

The largest synthesis of its kind on why teachers adopt AI, and what the standard model leaves out.

What we found
What we found
Across 38 studies covering nearly 16,000 teachers in 19 countries, the core Technology Acceptance Model relationships hold strongly, but results vary enormously by context. We propose TAM-I, an extension that adds teachers' professional identity and epistemic beliefs.
Why it matters
Leaders cannot rely on "AI is useful" messaging. Adoption depends on context and on what AI means for teachers' sense of their profession.

Why one AI workshop a year cannot work, and what replaces it

GenAI-TPD: a staged roadmap for teacher professional development in generative AI.

What we found
What we found
Synthesising 20 peer-reviewed studies, we propose an affordance-based model with five competency domains, four developmental stages and four affordance categories: functional, cognitive, social and reflective.
Why it matters
It replaces one-off tool training with a progression that schools can plan, sequence and assess. Other researchers are already applying it, including in secondary language classrooms in China.

What we found

Anticipate IEEE ACDSA 2027 Accepted
4

Do AI tutors actually teach, or just answer? We tested four.

Good teachers fade their support as a learner improves. We checked whether AI tutors do the same.

What we found
What we did
We probed four commercial AI tutoring modes with a scripted learner who struggles, gains competence and then regresses, comparing tutoring mode on and off and a purpose-built tutoring agent. Responses were blind double coded for directness.
Why it matters
It sets a baseline for judging future agentic tutors against a core principle of good teaching. An AI tutor that never lets go is not teaching. It is answering.
Forthcoming, February 2027
Govern IEEE ACDSA 2026 Published
6

The world's largest AI education push, scored on six dimensions. One fails.

India's national AI literacy initiatives, benchmarked against UNESCO and OECD standards.

What we found
What we found
Policy framing and student competencies are strong. Ethics and governance, teacher professional development and implementation are partial. Data protection is the weakest dimension. High-risk deployments such as dropout prediction, biometric attendance and learning analytics run without adequate safeguards.
Why it matters
National ambition is outpacing governance, and teacher preparation is a bottleneck. Any large system can check itself against the same six dimensions.

A compliance document cannot see what a student actually experiences

Why AI regulation in education needs ethics built into the tool itself.

What we found
What we found
Post-hoc regulatory governance, as in the EU AI Act, and design-embedded ethical constraints work best as complementary layers, with explainability treated as a property of the interaction rather than a retrospective obligation.
Why it matters
Schools and vendors should ask not only "is this compliant?" but "does this tool explain itself to teachers when it matters?"

Build IEEE ACDSA 2027 Accepted
90 min

What does one 90-minute workshop actually change?

44 teachers in Bengaluru, measured before and after a hands-on generative AI session.

What we found
What we did
44 teachers in Bengaluru used generative AI to design curriculum materials in a hands-on workshop. AI literacy readiness, measured with Technology Acceptance Model constructs, was higher after the workshop than before.
Why it matters
Short, practice-based sessions can move readiness in a large, AI-first education system.

2

Teaching a teacher to use AI is quick. Teaching them to doubt it is not.

Training teachers not only to use AI, but to judge it, using two scaffolds: RTCO and TRUST.

What we found
What we did
A two-site professional development intervention with school teachers in Karnataka, introducing two scaffolds: RTCO for constructing prompts and TRUST for verifying what AI produces.
Why it matters
Prompting changes with every model release. Judgement about when to trust output, how to verify it and when to set it aside is what lasts.

Universities are launching AI credentials they cannot yet assess

A scoping review of 82 studies from 26 countries, and a maturity model for judging your own provision.

What we found
What we found
A PRISMA-ScR scoping review of 82 studies from 26 countries. Most programmes are still at proposal or pilot stage, few specify measurable learning outcomes, and many do not document how ethics is taught.
Why it matters
We propose an AI Credential Maturity Model, levels L0 to L3, to help institutions judge where their own provision actually stands.

Where I stand

Research should end in a point of view

Judgement, not prompts

Prompting is the easy part, and it changes with every model release. What lasts is knowing when to trust AI output, how to verify it, and when it has no place in learning.

Identity matters more than usefulness

The usual pitch is that AI saves time. The adoption evidence points to something the standard models miss: what AI means for a teacher's professional identity. Training that ignores identity meets quiet resistance.

What is not measured is not improved

Teacher AI literacy has many frameworks and few validated instruments. Every serious programme should diagnose readiness before training and measure change after it.

Governance belongs inside the tool

Regulation after deployment is necessary but not sufficient. Educational AI should explain itself to teachers and learners at the moment of use.
