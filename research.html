The research programme

Five strands, one question

My research asks what it takes for teachers to work with AI with professional judgement. I pursue that question through five connected strands. Each one produces findings that stand alone, and together they form a single argument: teacher AI literacy must be defined clearly, explained in context, built through practice, governed responsibly, and kept current as AI itself changes.

01 Define

What AI literacy means for teachers

The field is not short of AI literacy frameworks. It is short of agreement. My umbrella review synthesised 28 systematic reviews and found fragmented theoretical foundations, an absence of standardised assessment instruments, and little attention to explainability, the teacher's ability to understand why an AI system produced what it did.

From that foundation I developed the GenAI-TPD framework, published in Discover Education. It synthesises 20 peer-reviewed studies into an affordance-based model for teacher professional development: five competency domains, four developmental stages, and four categories of affordance that generative AI offers teachers (functional, cognitive, social and reflective). The point is progression. Teachers do not become AI-literate in one workshop; they move through stages, and professional development should be designed for where they are.

The framework is already being used beyond my own studies. In a design-based research project led by Deng Lan in an English-as-a-foreign-language classroom in Shenzhen, China, GenAI-TPD provides the critical lens for investigating how teachers and AI divide the work of second-language writing instruction.

Key outputs
  • Umbrella review, HICSS 59, 2026
  • GenAI-TPD framework, Discover Education, 2026
  • GenAI-TPD applied in China (Deng, Shenoy & Saarela), ICETC 2026, accepted
02 Explain

Why teachers adopt AI, or don't

The Technology Acceptance Model is the most common lens for studying teacher adoption. My meta-analysis and systematic review in Artificial Intelligence Review tested how its core constructs (perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and behavioural intention) hold up in AI adoption, pooling evidence from nearly 16,000 teachers across 19 countries.

The core relationships are strong. But results vary enormously from one context to another, which tells us that context, not technology alone, decides adoption. The standard model also misses something teachers care about deeply. I proposed TAM-I, an extension that adds teachers' professional identity and epistemic beliefs: what teachers believe their role is, and how they believe knowledge is made. For leaders, the implication is direct. Telling teachers that AI is useful is not enough. Professional development has to engage with what AI means for who teachers are.

My Indian field studies extend this with readiness and usability evidence from teacher training contexts, including a Technology Acceptance Model study of generative AI usability in teacher training (ICECCME 2026) and a study diagnosing teachers' readiness before training is designed (ICETC 2026).

Key outputs
  • TAM meta-analysis, Artificial Intelligence Review, 2026
  • Generative AI usability in teacher training, ICECCME 2026, accepted
  • Diagnosing readiness before designing training, ICETC 2026, accepted
03 Build

What actually develops teacher AI literacy

Frameworks matter only if they change practice. I design and test interventions in real schools. In one study, a 90-minute, hands-on workshop in which teachers used generative AI to design curriculum materials was followed by higher AI literacy readiness among the 44 participating teachers in Bengaluru (accepted at ACDSA 2027). A two-site study with teacher cohorts in Karnataka examines how training can build both adoption readiness and the habit of critically evaluating AI output, using two scaffolds I developed: RTCO, for constructing prompts, and TRUST, for verifying what AI produces (under review).

To measure readiness consistently, I built aittt.app (AI Train The Teacher), a research instrument used in my published studies and made freely available to the research community. Its companion, SCALE (Scaffolding Classroom AI Literacy for Educators), is an open practice toolkit grounded in the GenAI-TPD framework.

Key outputs
  • Generative AI intervention study, IEEE ACDSA 2027, accepted
  • Designing for critical interaction (RTCO and TRUST scaffolds), under review
  • aittt.app research instrument
  • SCALE practice toolkit
04 Govern

How schools and systems should govern AI

Teachers do not adopt AI in a vacuum. They work inside policy. My benchmark study of India's national AI literacy initiatives (NITI Aayog, the Ministry of Education and CBSE), measured against UNESCO and OECD standards, found strong policy framing and student competencies, partial coverage of ethics, governance, teacher professional development and implementation, and very weak attention to data protection. It also documented high-risk uses, such as dropout prediction, biometric attendance and learning analytics, running without adequate governance safeguards.

With Dr. Mirka Saarela, I have argued that regulation after the fact, as in the EU AI Act, needs a partner: ethics and explainability designed into the tool itself and visible at the moment of use. We call this the shift from post-hoc regulation to runtime governance.

Governance also reaches higher education. A scoping review of AI credentials, now under review, maps how universities are building AI programmes and proposes an AI Credential Maturity Model (levels L0 to L3) to help institutions judge where their own provision stands.

Key outputs
  • India AI literacy policy benchmark, IEEE ACDSA 2026
  • Runtime governance, IEEE ICHORA 2026
  • AI credentials scoping review, ACM Transactions on Computing Education, under review
05 Anticipate

What agentic AI means for learning

AI is moving from tools that answer to agents that act, including AI tutors that guide learners through problems. The most cited theory in this area is scaffolding, but good scaffolding is contingent: it fades as the learner gains competence and returns when they struggle. My study accepted at ACDSA 2027 tests whether four commercial AI tutoring modes actually behave this way, using a scripted learner who struggles, succeeds and then regresses, with blind double coding by two raters. It establishes a baseline against which future agentic tutors can be judged.

This strand also includes work in progress on how AI tutors should remember learners (accountable pedagogical memory), a proposal for educationally aligned AI that optimises for durable learning rather than instant answers (LEARN), and a field study of whether teachers defer to AI judgements.

Key outputs
  • Contingent scaffolding in commercial AI tutors, IEEE ACDSA 2027, accepted
  • Ongoing work: accountable pedagogical memory, LEARN, teacher deference
Current thinking

Where I stand

Research should end in a point of view. These are the positions my work has led me to. Each is grounded in evidence, and each is open to being tested and revised.

1. Judgement, not prompts

Most AI training for teachers teaches prompting. Prompting is the easy part, and it changes with every model release. What lasts is judgement: knowing when to trust AI output, how to verify it, and when it has no place in learning. AI literacy for teachers should be built around evaluation, not instruction-writing.

Evidence: GenAI-TPD framework; critical interaction study (RTCO and TRUST scaffolds).

2. What is not measured is not improved

Teacher AI literacy has many frameworks and few validated instruments. Without measurement, systems cannot tell whether professional development works. Every serious AI literacy programme should diagnose readiness before training and measure change after it.

Evidence: umbrella review; readiness diagnosis study; aittt.app.

3. Professional development must become continuous

AI capabilities change every few months. A workshop twice a year cannot keep pace. Teacher learning about AI has to become small, frequent and woven into everyday work, while remaining staged, so that teachers progress rather than repeat the basics.

Evidence: GenAI-TPD developmental stages.

4. Identity matters more than usefulness

The usual pitch to teachers is that AI saves time. The adoption evidence shows that usefulness matters, but it also points to something the standard models miss: what AI means for a teacher's professional identity and beliefs about knowledge. Training that ignores identity meets quiet resistance.

Evidence: TAM meta-analysis and the TAM-I extension.

5. Context decides

Adoption results vary enormously between contexts. Frameworks built in high-resource systems cannot simply be exported. That is why I study India and Finland side by side: to separate what is universal from what is local.

Evidence: TAM meta-analysis; India and Finland studies.

6. Governance belongs inside the tool

Regulation after deployment is necessary but not sufficient. Educational AI should explain itself to teachers and learners at the moment of use, and systems running high-risk applications, such as dropout prediction or biometric attendance, need safeguards before deployment, not after.

Evidence: runtime governance paper; India policy benchmark.

7. An AI tutor that never lets go is not teaching

Good teachers fade their support as students gain competence. If AI tutors answer every request at the same level, they risk building dependence instead of understanding. AI tutoring should be judged by whether it adapts to the learner, and teachers need the literacy to tell the difference.

Evidence: contingent scaffolding study.

8. AI should be aligned to learning, not to satisfaction

General-purpose AI is optimised to be helpful: to give the most satisfying answer, fast. In education that can mean shallow understanding and dependence. Educational AI should be aligned to durable learning, through calibrated guidance, retrieval, self-explanation and productive struggle.

Evidence: LEARN proposal, in development.

The question is no longer whether teachers use AI. It is whether they use it with judgement.
Where this is going

The change I am working toward

Every education system is now making decisions about AI and teachers, often faster than the evidence. My aim is to make sure those decisions rest on research, and that teachers are treated as professionals exercising judgement, not as users to be trained on tools.

Every teacher able to evaluate AI, not just operate it

Judgement about when to trust, verify or reject AI output becomes a core professional competence, assessed like any other.

Teacher AI literacy that is measured

Systems diagnose readiness before training and measure change after it, using shared, validated instruments.

Professional development that keeps pace with AI

Continuous, staged and context-sensitive, rather than occasional and generic.

AI in education that is governed by design

Tools that explain themselves at the moment of use, and policies that protect learners' data before high-risk systems are deployed.

Research agenda, 2027 to 2030

  • Complete the cross-national evidence base. Finish the India and Finland comparison and validate the GenAI-TPD framework and the TAM-I extension empirically in both systems.
  • Move from self-report to behaviour. Study what teachers actually do with AI, how they grade with it, edit it and verify it, not only what they say about it.
  • Hold AI tutors to teaching standards. Extend the contingent scaffolding baseline into a repeatable evaluation method for agentic tutors, including how they remember and model learners.
  • Understand student dependence. Investigate how students offload thinking to AI, and how teacher AI literacy can protect learning.
  • Align AI with learning. Develop and test the LEARN principles for educationally aligned AI.
  • Widen the map. Extend the research to more education systems, particularly under-resourced and under-represented teachers in the Global South.

If your school, system, university or foundation is working on any of these questions, I would like to hear from you.

How the work is done

Methods I use

  • Umbrella reviews, systematic reviews and scoping reviews (PRISMA 2020, PRISMA-ScR)
  • Random-effects meta-analysis
  • Structured policy document analysis with benchmarking rubrics and inter-rater reliability checks
  • Field interventions with pre and post measurement
  • Structured behavioural probes of AI systems with blind double coding
  • Survey instrument development and structural equation modelling
  • Cross-national qualitative comparison (India and Finland)
